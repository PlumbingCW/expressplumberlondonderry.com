Two things shape plumbing here. Winter cold stresses pipes in unheated spaces, and many homes have moved from private wells to a public water line, which means new connections and new service lines. Both lead to emergency calls, so this page covers them in order.

Winter first: freezing pipes and what to do

New Hampshire winters freeze exposed pipes in garages, crawlspaces and outside walls. A pipe that is frozen but not yet split can often be thawed, and one that has split floods when the ice melts. Open the faucet, then shut the main valve if you see water or a bulge in the pipe.

Our burst pipe repair page covers frozen pipes and what happens when they split. Do not use an open flame on a frozen pipe. Heat from a hair dryer, moved slowly from the faucet back toward the freeze, is safer, and if you cannot find the frozen spot, call the 24/7 line.

Wells, PFOA and the move to public water

In 2016 the state and the company behind the former Saint-Gobain plant announced water line extensions for Litchfield. Private wells serving 124 homes and businesses had tested at or above 70 parts per trillion of PFOA, and 236 nearby wells brought the total to up to 360 properties able to connect to the public system through Pennichuck.

That history matters for plumbing today. A home that connected to public water has a new service line and shutoff, and a home that stayed on a well has a pump and pressure tank. If you are on a well, test your tap water and ask the state environmental department about testing programs. If you have connected, the line from the street to your house is your responsibility.

When that service line leaks, you see a wet spot in the yard, a meter that spins with every fixture off, or low pressure. Our water service line repair page explains how we find and fix the break, and leak detection helps when the wet spot is not obvious.
