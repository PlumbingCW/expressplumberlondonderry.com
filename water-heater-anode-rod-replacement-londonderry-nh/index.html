How Often to Replace a Water Heater Anode Rod in Londonderry, NH

The anode rod is the cheap part that protects the expensive tank. Here is how to check it and when to swap it.

By the Londonderry Express Plumbing team · Published 2026

Call (603) 815-9179

An anode rod is a metal rod inside a tank water heater that corrodes so the steel tank does not. Most manufacturers and plumbers suggest checking it every two to three years and replacing it once the steel core shows or the rod is thinner than a pencil. A fresh rod can add years to a tank, and a spent one lets rust start on the tank walls.

Replacing a rod is a modest job when the fittings cooperate, and a stubborn one when they do not. This guide covers how the rod works, how to inspect it, the rod types, and when it makes more sense to replace the whole heater. If your tank is already leaking, skip ahead to our water heater replacement page.

What the anode rod actually does

Water is slightly corrosive to steel. The rod is made of a more reactive metal, usually magnesium or aluminum, so it gives up its material first. This is called sacrificial protection.

The rod hangs from the top of the tank and reaches nearly to the bottom. As it corrodes, it sheds particles, and when it is gone the tank lining and steel take over the job. Rust-colored water, a smell of rotten eggs or a popping tank are all reasons to look at the rod.

How to tell if the rod is spent, and when to call

A plumber pulls the rod and looks at it. You can do the same if you are comfortable shutting off the power or gas, closing the cold supply and draining a few gallons. A rod that still looks like a fat metal stick has life left.

  • The steel core wire is visible along more than six inches of the rod.
  • The rod is thinner than about a half inch along most of its length.
  • Chunks of the coating are missing or the rod is coated in hard white scale.
  • The rod has not been checked in more than three years.
  • The water has started to smell or look discolored.

If the rod is in good shape, put it back with fresh thread sealant, restore the water, refill the tank before turning the power back on, and write the date on the tank for next time.

Which rod is right depends on your water chemistry and your tank's warranty terms. A plumber who knows your tank can match the rod. Do not pick a rod from a store shelf without checking the label on your heater.

Why the job can be hard

The rod hex head is usually 1-1/16 inch and sits on top of the tank, sometimes under a plastic cap. Rods installed years ago can be seized in place by corrosion. A long breaker bar or an impact wrench is often needed.

A rigid rod can be about three to four feet long, so you need that much clearance above the tank, or a flexible rod that bends. In a low closet or beneath a finished ceiling a flexible or segmented rod is the usual answer.

If the hex head rounds off or the tank threads strip, stop. Forcing it risks cracking the tank, and at that point an anode rod replacement is no longer the right fix.

Rod swap or new water heater?

A new rod buys time only if the tank is sound. If the heater is more than about ten years old, shows rust at the base or has a damp floor underneath, a rod will not save it. Leaks around the tank bottom mean the steel has failed.

Swap the rod

The tank is under ten years old, dry underneath and still heats evenly. A rod costs far less than a heater.

Flush and swap

Sediment is building up. Draining the tank while the rod is out improves efficiency and gets rid of rattling.

Replace the heater

The tank leaks, the rod threads are stripped or the tank is past its expected life. See water heater repair for the checks we run first.

Consider tankless

A rod does nothing for a tankless unit. If you are rethinking the system, see tankless water heater options.

Londonderry, NH note: town water, wells and your rod

Many Londonderry households draw from private wells, and the water can differ from house to house. No single hardness figure applies across town, so test your tap and check your result before choosing a rod. If you are on town water, ask your water utility for its annual water quality report.

Well owners in the area have been advised to test for PFAS and other contaminants through the New Hampshire Department of Environmental Services programs. Those tests do not choose your rod, but they tell you what is in the water your heater holds, and a plumber can match the rod to the chemistry you find.

The relief valve and discharge pipe on any tank follow the International Plumbing Code (IPC).
