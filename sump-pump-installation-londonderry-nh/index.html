Sump Pump Installation: A Londonderry, NH Homeowner Guide

What goes into a sump pump installation, which pump type fits your basement, and what to ask before you hire.

By the Londonderry Express Plumbing team · Published 2026

Call (603) 815-9179

A sump pump installation puts a collection pit in the lowest part of your basement or crawlspace, sets a pump in it and runs a discharge pipe outside. When groundwater rises, the pump moves water out before it spreads across the floor. The job is simple in concept, but the details decide whether it works when a storm hits.

This guide explains the pump types, the parts around the pump that matter as much as the pump itself, and the questions to ask any contractor. We do not install foundation drainage systems as a standalone product, so for the plumbing side of a sump setup we can help through our plumbing installation team, and for tracing basement water we use leak detection.

Do you actually need a sump pump?

Not every wet basement needs a pump. Water in a basement can come from a high water table, roof and yard runoff, a leaking supply line, a failed floor drain or a sewer backup. A pump only helps the first two.

Before a pit is cut into your floor, rule out plumbing sources. A meter that keeps spinning with everything off, a hissing wall or a damp patch that appears in dry weather all point to a leak and not groundwater. A plumber can check that in under an hour.

  • Water appears along the wall or floor joint after heavy rain or snowmelt.
  • The basement smells musty and the floor stays damp for days.
  • A floor crack weeps in spring.
  • Stored items on the floor show water lines.
  • A neighbor on a similar lot already runs a pump.

A submersible is the usual choice for a home that sees real water. A pedestal pump suits a small crawlspace or a light, occasional flood. Ask your installer why they picked the one they did, and what its flow rating is in gallons per hour at your discharge height.

The parts around the pump

A pump alone is not a system. The pit liner, lid, float switch, check valve and discharge pipe determine how reliably the water leaves.

Pit and liner

A pit with a solid liner and a sealed lid keeps debris and radon-laden soil air out of the basement.

Float switch

This tells the pump when to start and stop. A float that snags the side of the pit is the number one cause of pump failure.

Check valve

It stops water in the discharge pipe from running back into the pit after the pump shuts off.

Discharge pipe

It should carry water well away from the foundation and not end up where it can run back toward the house.

The outlet matters too. Use a dedicated ground-fault protected outlet, and do not run the pump from an extension cord. A power outage during a storm is when you need the pump most, which is why we talk about battery backup below.

Battery backup and alarms

Storms knock out power, and a pump with no power does nothing. A battery backup pump sits beside the main pump and takes over when the power drops or the primary fails. A water level alarm adds a warning if the pit fills anyway.

A backup is not a generator. A battery gives hours of pumping in a light flood, not days. If you lose power often, ask about a larger battery bank or a generator transfer plan.

Where the water goes

The discharge line needs a destination that will not send water back to your own basement or into your neighbor's yard. Pipe it to a downhill part of the lot and spread it with a splash block or a buried extension.

Do not connect a sump pump to a sanitary sewer line or a septic tank. Pumped groundwater can overload a septic system, and many towns prohibit sump connections to the sewer. Ask your installer how your lot handles the discharge, and ask the town if you are unsure.

What sump pump installation costs

Sump work is quoted before it starts. The bill is driven by:

  • Whether a new pit must be cut into a concrete slab or an existing pit is reused.
  • The pump type, horsepower and whether a backup is added.
  • Discharge pipe length, the number of bends and where the water ends up.
  • Electrical work for a ground-fault outlet and a dedicated circuit.
  • After-hours or emergency dispatch.

Homeowners insurance often excludes groundwater and sump failure unless an endorsement was added, so check your policy. For a real number, call (603) 815-9179.

Londonderry, NH note: drainage and permits

Londonderry sits on the Merrimack River watershed. Beaver Brook drains the east and south parts of town, and Little Cohas, Watts, Colby and Nesenkeag Brooks drain the west. Homes near those brooks and at the base of rises such as Number Eight Hill may see more groundwater after storms, so ask neighbors on your street whether their basements stay dry.

Before you cut a floor or add a new circuit, call the town Building and Zoning Enforcement desk (603-432-1100, extension 440), since what needs a permit changes with the job. Water coming off a road or ditch is a question for Public Works, extension 460, which can say who owns the drainage.

Where a sump discharges is a code question, and plumbing portions follow the International Plumbing Code (IPC).
